Calcium indicators

How a flash of green light reports that a neuron just fired

Howard Wang · Ting-Ruei Liu
Rice University · November 30, 2026

Hi everyone, we’re Howard and Ting-Ruei, and our topic is calcium indicators. The first group explained how fluorescence works. We pick up from there and ask: how do you make a fluorescent molecule whose brightness depends on neural activity? The most widely used answer is to watch calcium. We’ll cover why calcium follows spiking, how the GCaMP sensor works, how it compares with chemical dyes, and then the limits: photobleaching, slow kinetics, and what the sensor does to the cell.

Why calcium? Because calcium entry is tightly coupled to the action potential. When a neuron spikes, the depolarization opens voltage-gated calcium channels and calcium flows in. The key is the size of the change. At rest, free calcium inside the cell is extremely low, about 50 to 100 nanomolar, while outside it is one to two millimolar. That is roughly a ten-thousand-fold gradient. So even a brief channel opening raises cytosolic calcium ten to a hundred times above baseline. Voltage itself changes by a factor of about two during a spike; calcium changes by orders of magnitude. That large, spike-triggered swing is what makes calcium such a convenient thing to measure.

Sources: Nakai et al., Nat. Biotechnol. (2001); Akerboom et al., J. Biol. Chem. (2009); Chen et al., Nature (2013)

GCaMP is one protein made of three parts fused together. In the middle is a circularly permuted GFP: the normal GFP barrel has been cut open and its ends re-joined, which leaves an opening right next to the chromophore. On one end is calmodulin, a natural calcium-binding protein, and on the other end is M13, a short peptide that calcium-bound calmodulin likes to grab. Without calcium, the two ends hang loose. Water reaches the chromophore, it stays mostly protonated, and it is dim under blue light. When calcium rises, calmodulin binds up to four calcium ions, wraps around M13, and the complex closes the opening. The chromophore becomes deprotonated and bright. It is reversible, so when calcium is pumped out the protein dims again.

Before GCaMP, the standard tools were synthetic dyes such as Oregon Green BAPTA, Fluo-4 and Fura-2. These are small molecules built on a calcium chelator. They are fast and fairly linear, and they are still used. But you have to physically load them, either by injecting a membrane-permeable form into tissue or through a pipette, and they label every cell nearby, neurons and glia alike. They also leak out within hours. Genetically encoded indicators solved those problems. Because the sensor is a gene, you can deliver it with a virus or a transgenic mouse, restrict it to a chosen cell type with a promoter or a Cre line, and return to the very same neurons for weeks or months. The price is that you wait for expression, the response is nonlinear, and both kinds of indicator buffer calcium.

The first limit is photobleaching, which group one introduced. For calcium imaging it matters in three ways. First, every time a sensor molecule is excited there is a small chance it is destroyed, so the raw fluorescence slowly falls through a session, as in this simulated trace. Second, that drift breaks the measurement if you ignore it. We report delta F over F, and F-zero is not a constant: it decays, and it decays at different rates in different cells and depths. So analysis pipelines estimate a running baseline. Third, it sets a light budget. Turning up the laser gives a cleaner signal now but bleaches faster and can damage tissue, so you trade signal-to-noise against how long you can record. One advantage of a genetically encoded sensor is that the cell keeps making new protein, so the signal recovers between sessions.

The second limit is speed. An action potential lasts about a millisecond. The calcium signal it produces does not. Calcium has to enter, bind the sensor, the protein has to change shape, and then calcium has to be pumped back out. With GCaMP6 the fluorescence takes roughly 50 to 150 milliseconds to reach its peak, depending on the variant, and then decays over hundreds of milliseconds. You can see the consequence in this simulation. A single spike gives a small bump. Three spikes close together do not give three bumps; they merge into one larger transient. A burst of six becomes one big hill. The newest generation, jGCaMP8, has a much faster onset, around two milliseconds to half-rise, which helps with timing, but the decay is still much longer than the spike itself. So the fluorescence trace is a smoothed, delayed version of the spike train.

Sources: Pnevmatikakis 2016; Friedrich 2017; Deneux 2016; Rupprecht 2021; Huang 2021

Because the indicator blurs the spike train, we often want to undo the blur. That is spike inference. The idea is that the measured trace is the hidden spike train passed through the indicator’s response and corrupted by noise, and we try to run that backwards. There are three families of methods. Deconvolution methods such as OASIS, which is used inside CNMF and Suite2p, assume every spike adds the same exponential transient. Generative models like MLspike fit drift, saturation and noise for each cell. And supervised methods like CASCADE train a neural network on ground-truth data where the same neuron was recorded electrically and imaged at once. The honest caveat: under typical population-imaging conditions, isolated single spikes are often missed, and during bursts the sensor saturates. So the output is best treated as an estimate of firing rate, not a list of exact spike times.

Biological concerns

Calcium buffering

The indicator binds the Ca²⁺ it reports. At high expression it competes with the cell’s own buffers and can shrink and slow real calcium signals.

Cell health

Long or strong expression can fill the nucleus, and those cells respond abnormally. GCaMP’s calmodulin can also disturb L-type Ca²⁺ channels; GCaMP-X shields it.

Development

Some GCaMP6 mouse lines that express from early life show epileptiform cortical events. Keeping expression off until about 7 weeks prevented them.

In practice: use the lowest expression that gives a signal, prefer validated lines, and exclude nucleus-filled cells.

Sources: Tian et al. (2009); Steinmetz et al. (2017); Yang et al. (2018); Gasterstädt et al. (2020)

A calcium indicator is not a passive observer, and there are three biological concerns. First, buffering. The sensor works by binding calcium, so it is itself a calcium buffer. If you express a lot of it, it competes with the cell’s own calcium-binding proteins and can make the real calcium signals smaller and slower. Second, cell health. With very strong or very long expression, GCaMP accumulates in the nucleus, and those filled cells show abnormal responses. Part of the reason is that GCaMP contains calmodulin, which can interfere with L-type calcium channels. A redesigned version called GCaMP-X shields the calmodulin to avoid this. Third, development. Several transgenic mouse lines that express GCaMP6 from early life show epilepsy-like events in cortex. When expression was kept off until about seven weeks of age, the events did not appear. So in practice: use the lowest expression that works, use validated lines, and exclude filled cells.

Tighter Ca²⁺ binding gives a bigger signal, but it lets go slowly and saturates sooner.

Sources: Chen et al., Nature (2013); Zhang et al., Nature (2023)

These limits lead to an engineering tradeoff, which is why indicators come in families. Each generation of GCaMP is released as a sensitive version, a medium version and a fast version: 6s, 6m and 6f, and now 8s, 8m and 8f. The underlying reason is calcium binding. If the sensor holds calcium tightly, you get a large response even to one spike, but it releases calcium slowly, so the signal decays slowly, and it saturates sooner during bursts. If it binds more loosely, it follows spike trains better but each spike gives a smaller signal, so you need more photons. So there is no single best indicator. You choose based on the question: if you need to know whether a sparsely firing neuron fired at all, pick a sensitive one; if you care about timing or fast firing, pick a fast one.

Take-home

1

Calcium is a proxy for spiking: spikes open Ca²⁺ channels, and GCaMP turns the Ca²⁺ rise into green light.

2

It is a slow, nonlinear proxy: expect firing rates, not exact spike times.

3

The sensor is not free: bleaching, buffering and expression all need controls.

To wrap up, three things to remember. One: calcium is a proxy for spiking. Spikes open calcium channels, calcium rises ten to a hundred fold, and GCaMP converts that rise into green fluorescence. Two: it is a slow and nonlinear proxy. The signal is delayed and smoothed, so what you can trust is firing rate, not exact spike times. That is the gap the next group’s topic, voltage imaging, is trying to close. Three: the sensor is not free. Photobleaching limits how long and how brightly you can image, and the protein itself buffers calcium and can affect the cell, so good experiments control for expression level.
